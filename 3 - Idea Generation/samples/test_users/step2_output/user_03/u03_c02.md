**User:** I've been generating ensembles from an ML weather model by perturbing the initial conditions, and the spread is way too small. The ensemble mean error is clearly larger than the ensemble spread at most lead times, so the forecasts are overconfident. Is this a known issue with data-driven models, and why would it happen?

**Assistant:** Yes, under-dispersion is a widely reported issue. Deterministic ML models trained with a mean-squared-error loss learn to predict something close to the conditional mean, which smooths out small-scale variability. When you perturb initial conditions, the model tends to damp those perturbations rather than let them grow chaotically the way the real atmosphere does. So trajectories converge rather than diverge. Physics-based ensembles also include model-error representations like stochastic parameterisations, which ML models lack by default. The result is a spread-skill ratio well below one, especially at medium-range lead times.

**User:** Right, the smoothing makes sense. My spread-skill ratio is around 0.5 at day five and gets worse after that. I'm trying to figure out whether to fix this inside the model, like training it to be probabilistic, or outside it with a post-processing calibration. Retraining is expensive for me so I'm leaning toward post-processing first. What are my options?

**Assistant:** Post-processing is a sensible first step. Options include ensemble model output statistics, which fits a parametric predictive distribution using the ensemble mean and spread as predictors with coefficients fitted to minimise the continuous ranked probability score; simple spread inflation with a lead-time-dependent factor; quantile regression or distributional regression with neural networks; and member-by-member methods that rescale deviations from the mean while preserving the ensemble structure. Member-by-member approaches are attractive if you need physically consistent spatial fields for downstream use, since they keep spatial correlations.
