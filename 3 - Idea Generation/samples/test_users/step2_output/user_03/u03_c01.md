**User:** I've been running some of the open data-driven global weather models and the headline skill scores are genuinely impressive. But I'm sceptical about one thing: they're trained on roughly forty years of reanalysis, so a heatwave that breaks the record by several degrees is by definition outside what they've seen. Is there any reason to expect a neural network to extrapolate correctly to that, or is it just going to regress toward the training distribution?

**Assistant:** Your scepticism is well founded and is an active research question. Neural networks generally interpolate well within the training distribution and can behave poorly outside it. That said, record-breaking events are often unusual combinations of familiar ingredients, such as a persistent blocking high, dry soils, and warm air advection, each of which appears in training data. If the model has learned the dynamics that combine these ingredients, it may produce extreme outcomes even without having seen that exact magnitude. Early case studies show mixed results: some ML models capture the large-scale pattern of record events but underestimate peak temperatures compared with physics-based models.

**User:** That matches my gut. The pattern is probably fine, the magnitude is where I'd expect them to fall short. I want to test this properly rather than relying on a couple of cherry-picked case studies. I'm thinking of running hindcasts for every event that set a regional temperature record in the held-out years and comparing peak error against a physics model. Does that design make sense?

**Assistant:** It is a solid design. A few suggestions: define events objectively, for instance as exceedance of the prior record at a gridpoint or regional average, so selection is not biased. Evaluate at multiple lead times, since ML models may degrade differently from physics models as lead time grows. Compare against a baseline that is equally out-of-sample, and make sure none of the test years leaked into training or validation. Finally, look at the error conditional on how far the event exceeded the previous record, because the key question is whether error grows with the degree of extrapolation.
