**User:** Extending my extremes analysis beyond heat. I looked at a few storms with really unusual tracks, like a tropical system that turned sharply poleward and reintensified, which is rare in the record. The ML model completely smoothed it into a weaker, more typical track at four days lead, while the physics model caught the turn. Is this the same out-of-distribution problem or something different?

**Assistant:** It is likely related but has extra elements. Rare tracks often depend on sensitive interactions, such as a tropical system phasing with a mid-latitude trough, which are underrepresented in training data. ML models trained with mean-squared-error loss also blur intense compact features, so even a well-predicted storm may appear weaker. Both the rarity and the smoothing push toward a typical, weaker outcome. Physics models resolve the interaction explicitly, so they can produce unusual tracks if initial conditions support it.

**User:** So for storms I have two confounded issues, smoothing and rarity. I'd like to disentangle them. Maybe compare against common tracks of similar intensity to isolate the rarity effect? I'm still pretty sceptical that these models are ready for warnings on the events that matter most, even though the average scores look great.

**Assistant:** Comparing rare-track storms against common-track storms of matched intensity is a good way to isolate rarity. You might also evaluate a probabilistic ML model trained with a proper scoring rule, which suffers less from smoothing, to see how much of the error remains. Your caution about warnings is shared by many operational centres, which currently treat ML forecasts as complementary guidance rather than a replacement for physics models in high-impact situations.
