**User:** Totally different topic from my usual work. A friend is building a small quadrotor drone and asked me about trajectory optimisation, since I do numerical stuff. From what I've read, minimum-snap polynomial trajectories are the standard. Why snap specifically, the fourth derivative of position? It seems arbitrary to me, and I'm not sure it's better than just minimising time or energy.

**Assistant:** It's less arbitrary than it looks. Quadrotors are differentially flat: position and yaw determine the full state and inputs. Thrust relates to acceleration, and the body angular rates relate to jerk, so angular acceleration, which is directly tied to motor torques, relates to snap. Minimising snap therefore smooths the motor commands, reducing abrupt rotor speed changes. It also turns into a convenient quadratic program over polynomial coefficients. Minimum-time formulations are popular for racing, but they push actuators to saturation and are nonconvex, so they're harder to solve reliably.

**User:** Oh, the differential flatness part makes it click. So snap is basically a proxy for how hard the motors are working. For a hobby drone that just needs to fly smoothly between waypoints, I'm guessing minimum-snap is plenty and the time allocation between segments is the harder part. Is that right?

**Assistant:** Yes, that's a good summary. For smooth waypoint flight minimum snap works well, and segment time allocation is indeed the trickier part, since bad allocations can make polynomials overshoot or violate velocity limits. Common approaches include heuristic allocation from distance and maximum speed, then iteratively scaling times until constraints are satisfied, or optimising times by gradient descent on total cost. Libraries for minimum-snap generation often include such routines, so your friend may not need to implement it from scratch.
