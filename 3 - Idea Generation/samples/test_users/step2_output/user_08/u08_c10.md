**User:** Back to my bone marrow data. I want to pinpoint where multipotent progenitors commit to either the erythroid-megakaryocyte side or the myeloid side. Visually there's a fork in the UMAP but I don't trust UMAP geometry. Is there a statistically grounded way to locate the bifurcation and identify genes that change right at the decision point, rather than just eyeballing a fork?

**Assistant:** Yes, avoid relying on UMAP. Options include computing fate probabilities toward each terminal state with tools like CellRank or Palantir, then defining the decision region where fate entropy is highest, meaning cells are still uncommitted, followed by where it drops. Branch-point detection methods in Slingshot-style or graph-based approaches also identify where lineage curves separate. For genes, test for differential expression between branches as a function of position near the split, for example with branch-aware generalised additive models such as those in tradeSeq.

**User:** The entropy idea is nice because it's quantitative. My worry is that the commitment point might not be a sharp fork at all, more like a gradual bias that builds up, in which case a 'branch point' is somewhat artificial. And I'd love to check whether the region I find matches where clones in our barcoding experiment start to become restricted to one fate.

**Assistant:** Your concern matches current thinking in hematopoiesis, where commitment is often described as a continuum of gradually increasing fate bias rather than discrete forks. You could report the fate probability gradient along the progression rather than a single point, and fit sigmoid curves to estimate where bias crosses a threshold, with confidence intervals by bootstrapping cells. Comparing with your clonal data is a strong test: if early cells whose clones are fate-restricted fall after your inferred decision region, and multipotent clones before it, that supports your localisation independently.
