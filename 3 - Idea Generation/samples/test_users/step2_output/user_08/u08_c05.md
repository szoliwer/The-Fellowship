**User:** Off-topic curiosity: I saw a figure of glacier surface velocity across an ice sheet made from satellite radar, and I'm curious how that works. I know InSAR measures phase differences between two radar images, but that only gives motion along the line of sight, right? How do they get full horizontal velocity fields out of that? It seems like you'd be missing a component.

**Assistant:** You're right that a single interferogram only measures line-of-sight displacement. To recover horizontal velocity, people combine ascending and descending passes, which view the surface from different directions, and often assume flow is parallel to the surface so the vertical component can be constrained from a digital elevation model. Another approach is offset tracking, which correlates image patches between acquisitions to measure displacement in both range and azimuth directly. It's less precise than phase methods but works for fast-moving ice where interferometric phase becomes incoherent.

**User:** That makes sense, so fast outlet glaciers probably rely on offset tracking and slow interior ice on the phase method. I'd guess melting and snowfall between acquisitions also wrecks coherence. Is that why some of the velocity maps have such big gaps?

**Assistant:** Exactly. Coherence loss from surface melting, snowfall, wind redistribution and very fast or shearing flow all cause gaps. Shorter repeat intervals help, which is why missions with frequent revisits improved coverage dramatically. Mosaics are typically built from many image pairs over a season or year to fill gaps, and some products blend radar with optical feature tracking. Your intuition about the split is right: interior ice uses phase methods for millimetre-level sensitivity, while fast margins rely on offset tracking.
