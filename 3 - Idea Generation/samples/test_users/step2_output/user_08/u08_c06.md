**User:** I got curious about how linguists build family trees of languages. I've seen papers using Bayesian phylogenetics on lists of basic vocabulary, coding words as cognate or not. Coming from biology, I'm skeptical that borrowing between languages doesn't wreck the tree assumption, since it seems like horizontal transfer would be way more common than in genes. How do they handle it?

**Assistant:** Borrowing is a major concern, and you're right that it's like horizontal gene transfer. Linguists mitigate it by using basic vocabulary lists, such as body parts, numbers and kinship terms, which are relatively resistant to borrowing, and by removing known loanwords during cognate coding. Some studies test how much undetected borrowing would bias results through simulations, finding that moderate borrowing mainly shortens tree branches and affects dates more than topology. Network methods like NeighborNet are also used to visualise conflicting signals rather than forcing a tree.

**User:** Interesting that borrowing hits dates more than the branching order. I'd still be suspicious of the dates in general, since the rate of word replacement surely isn't constant. Do these models still assume a strict clock like the old glottochronology did?

**Assistant:** No, modern approaches explicitly reject glottochronology's constant rate. They use relaxed clock models, allowing rates to vary across branches, and often let different meaning classes evolve at different rates. Dates are calibrated with historically attested split times, like documented ancient languages, which act like fossil calibrations in biology. Even so, your suspicion is shared: date estimates often have wide credible intervals, and results can be sensitive to calibration choices and the cognate coding scheme.
