**User:** I help coordinate a group of neighbourhood volunteers who've put up about forty low-cost sensors on balconies and fences. They log temperature, humidity and fine particulate matter every few minutes. People are really enthusiastic, but when I look at the raw data some units read two or three degrees warmer than their neighbours for no obvious reason. Before I go further, what are the common reasons cheap sensors disagree like this?

**Assistant:** Low-cost sensor disagreement usually comes from a few sources. Siting is the biggest one for temperature: a sensor in direct sun, against a south-facing wall, or without a radiation shield will read high during the day. Unit-to-unit manufacturing variation adds a fixed offset, often a degree or so. Self-heating from electronics in a closed enclosure can bias readings upward. For particulate sensors, humidity is a major issue, because optical counters see swollen water-coated particles and overestimate mass at high relative humidity. Finally, there is drift, where a sensor's response slowly changes over months as components age or get dirty.

**User:** Okay, siting is probably part of it, we gave people instructions but I doubt everyone followed them. Honestly though, even when I compare two units sitting side by side on my own balcony they diverge over a couple of months. So I think we have both fixed offsets and drift. Is there a simple fix, or is this a real statistics problem?

**Assistant:** Side-by-side divergence over months points to genuine drift, and that is more than a one-off fix. A common first step is a co-location period: put all units next to a reference-grade instrument for a week or two, fit a per-unit correction, then deploy. But drift means that correction decays, so you need periodic re-co-location or a statistical method that tracks drift over time, for example using a reference station as an anchor and modelling each sensor's bias as a slowly varying function. It is a well-studied but genuinely nontrivial problem.
