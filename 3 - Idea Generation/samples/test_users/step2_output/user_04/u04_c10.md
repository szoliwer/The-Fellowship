**User:** I'm drafting a short memo for the city's urban forestry team arguing that canopy targets should be set per neighbourhood instead of a single citywide percentage. A citywide target of thirty percent can be met while the hottest, lowest-income areas stay at ten. I want to tie the argument to the cooling evidence and the planting history. Can you help me structure it?

**Assistant:** A clear structure could be: first, the problem, showing that the current citywide average masks a wide range across neighbourhoods; second, why it matters, summarising evidence that cooling benefits are nonlinear and concentrated above certain canopy thresholds, so averages are misleading; third, the historical pattern, showing that past plantings went disproportionately to already-green areas and that survival may be lower in harsher sites; fourth, the proposal, neighbourhood-level minimum canopy targets prioritised by heat exposure and vulnerability; and finally implementation, including proactive planting, maintenance funding for early establishment, and monitoring surviving canopy rather than planting counts.

**User:** That's close to what I had in mind. I'm unsure how strongly to state the cooling numbers, since the honest range is maybe one or two degrees of air temperature, which sounds small to a non-expert. I don't want to oversell it, but I also think even one degree matters for heat-related illness on the worst days.

**Assistant:** You can present the modest number honestly and then translate it. Explain that one to two degrees of air temperature is the average effect, while shaded pedestrians experience much larger reductions in radiant heat, which strongly affects thermal comfort. You can also note that health risk rises steeply on the hottest days, so small temperature reductions during extreme events can matter disproportionately. Being upfront about the uncertainty will make the memo more credible to technical readers.
