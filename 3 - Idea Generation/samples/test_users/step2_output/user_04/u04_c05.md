**User:** Another question on the satellite versus street-level problem. The thermal imagery I have comes from a mid-morning overpass, but the heat risk for people is mostly late afternoon and overnight. I have a handful of air temperature loggers from a city pilot, and they show the hottest blocks at 5pm aren't always the hottest blocks in the morning image. How much should I trust a morning snapshot for this?

**Assistant:** Your loggers are showing a real effect. Surface heating in the morning is driven by sun angle and surface properties, so east-facing and dark surfaces heat first. By late afternoon, accumulated heat storage in buildings and pavements, plus reduced ventilation in dense street canyons, can shift the hotspots. At night the pattern can change again, with dense built-up areas retaining heat while open green areas cool quickly. A single morning image captures one phase of that cycle. If possible, check whether any afternoon or night-time thermal acquisitions exist, and use your loggers to quantify how well the morning pattern ranks blocks at the times that matter for health.

**Assistant:** A rank correlation of 0.5 suggests the morning image captures part of the pattern but misses a lot, so your conclusion is reasonable. One option is to use the night-time scene for the cloud-free areas only, since night-time surface and air temperatures tend to agree more closely than daytime ones. Another is to treat LST as one predictor among several rather than as the exposure layer itself. Either way, documenting that 0.5 figure in your methods is valuable, because it shows readers why you did not rely on the satellite data alone.
