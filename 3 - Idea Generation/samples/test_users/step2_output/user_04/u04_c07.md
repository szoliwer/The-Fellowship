**User:** I've been mapping where new street trees were planted over the last decade, and it's pretty clear they went disproportionately to neighbourhoods that already had decent canopy and higher incomes. Part of it seems to be that the planting program was request-based, so residents had to ask. I want to make the equity argument carefully rather than just showing a map that looks bad. How would you frame this analytically?

**Assistant:** A careful framing would separate distribution of outcomes from the mechanism. First, quantify the disparity: plantings per kilometre of street or per available planting site, stratified by income or vulnerability quintile, with confidence intervals. Second, test the mechanism: if the program was request-based, compare request rates across neighbourhoods and the approval rate given a request. If requests drive the disparity, the policy lever is outreach or switching to proactive planting. Also account for physical constraints like sidewalk width and overhead wires, since critics will argue some neighbourhoods simply have fewer plantable sites.

**User:** Good point about plantable sites, the narrow sidewalks in the older dense areas are a real constraint. I also wonder whether tree survival differs, because planting a tree that dies in two years isn't really canopy. I'd like to estimate surviving canopy gain by neighbourhood rather than just counts of plantings. Is there usually data for that?

**Assistant:** Sometimes. Many city forestry departments keep an inventory with planting date and status updates, and some record removals. If that is incomplete, you can estimate survival by comparing planting locations against later high-resolution imagery or lidar canopy maps to see whether a crown developed. Survival is often lower in low-income areas due to harsher sites, less watering, and more vandalism or construction damage, which would compound the equity gap. Presenting surviving canopy gain per resident by neighbourhood would be a strong, defensible metric.
