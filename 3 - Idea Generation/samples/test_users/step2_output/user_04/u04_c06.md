**User:** I'm still fighting with drift in our volunteer temperature sensors. Some units seem to slowly read warmer over the summer and then come back in autumn, which makes me think it's partly dust on the radiation shields and partly seasonal sun angle. I tried subtracting a monthly offset relative to the reference station but it feels hacky and I'm not confident I'm not just removing real local differences. How do people separate drift from genuine local signal?

**Assistant:** That is the core identifiability challenge. If you correct every sensor to match the reference, you erase the real spatial differences you care about. Common strategies separate timescales: real local differences tend to show up as consistent diurnal patterns tied to land cover, while drift is slow and monotonic or seasonal. Methods include modelling each sensor's bias as a smooth function of time with a penalty, using periods of strong wind and well-mixed conditions as calibration windows when true spatial differences should be small, and rotating a travelling reference unit around the network. Hierarchical Bayesian models can formalise this by sharing information across sensors.

**User:** The well-mixed windy periods idea is clever, I hadn't thought of that. But I'm out of my depth with hierarchical Bayesian anything. This is really the bottleneck for us. The data are only useful if people trust them, and right now I can't defend the corrections. I need a collaborator who actually does measurement-error statistics.

**Assistant:** That is a reasonable conclusion, and it is common for community monitoring projects to partner with a statistician at exactly this stage. You might frame the collaboration around a clear deliverable, such as a documented, reproducible correction pipeline with uncertainty estimates for each sensor-hour. That gives the methods person a publishable contribution and gives your volunteers data they can defend. Meanwhile, keep logging metadata like shield cleaning dates and any relocations, since those events are extremely valuable for separating drift from real signal.
