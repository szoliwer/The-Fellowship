**User:** I'm building a heat-vulnerability map for a mid-sized city and the standard approach seems to be pulling land-surface temperature from a thermal satellite band and overlaying it with census vulnerability indicators. But I keep reading that surface temperature isn't what people actually experience. Is the difference between land-surface temperature and air temperature big enough that it would change which neighbourhoods get flagged as hotspots?

**Assistant:** It can be, yes. Land-surface temperature measures the skin temperature of whatever the sensor sees: rooftops, asphalt, tree crowns. On a sunny afternoon a dark roof can be 30 degrees hotter than the air two metres above the street. Air temperature at pedestrian height is smoothed by mixing and advection, so its spatial pattern is much flatter. The ranking of neighbourhoods often stays broadly similar, but the magnitude of differences shrinks, and some places flip, for example areas with lots of flat industrial roofs look extreme in LST but may be only modestly warmer at street level. Overpass timing also matters, since most thermal satellites pass in the morning, not at peak afternoon heat.

**User:** That's exactly my worry. The industrial zone on the east side lights up like a furnace in my LST layer, but almost nobody lives there, while some dense residential blocks with lots of shade look mild. I don't want health outreach going to the wrong place because I picked the convenient dataset. What's the honest way to choose?

**Assistant:** A pragmatic approach is to treat LST as a proxy and test how well it predicts air temperature in your city before relying on it. If you can find any street-level air temperature observations, even a few dozen points from a mobile traverse or fixed loggers, regress air temperature on LST plus land-cover variables like canopy fraction and building density. If the fit is weak or biased in particular land-cover types, you can either model air temperature directly or present LST with clear caveats. Many cities also combine an exposure layer with a population layer so that unoccupied hot surfaces are down-weighted in the final vulnerability index.
