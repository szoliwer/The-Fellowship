**User:** I think I've convinced myself that for the vulnerability map I should be estimating air temperature, not using land-surface temperature directly. My plan is to train a model on the pilot loggers using LST, canopy fraction, building height and distance to water as predictors, then predict across the city. But I only have about thirty loggers. Is that enough, or am I going to overfit and produce a pretty map that's mostly noise?

**Assistant:** Thirty loggers is limited but workable if you keep the model simple and validate honestly. With few stations, a regularised linear model or a generalised additive model with a handful of predictors is safer than a flexible machine-learning model. Use spatial cross-validation, leaving out clusters of nearby loggers, because random cross-validation overstates skill when stations are close together. Also check whether your loggers cover the full range of conditions in the city; if none sit in industrial zones, predictions there are extrapolations. Reporting a prediction uncertainty map alongside the estimate is good practice.

**User:** Yeah, coverage is a problem, none of the pilot loggers are in the industrial east side or the newest suburbs. So the model would basically be guessing there. I suppose I could flag those areas as low confidence. I still find it frustrating that the satellite data covers everything but measures the wrong thing, and the ground data measures the right thing but covers almost nothing.

**Assistant:** That tension is exactly why fusion approaches exist: the satellite provides spatial coverage and pattern, and ground data provide the anchor for what people experience. Flagging low-confidence areas is honest and useful. If you can add even a few temporary loggers in the unsampled land-cover types during the next hot season, the gain in confidence would likely be disproportionate, because you would be filling the gaps the model cannot extrapolate across.
