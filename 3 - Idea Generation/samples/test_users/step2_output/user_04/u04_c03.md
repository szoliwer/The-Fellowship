**User:** I'm trying to get a realistic number for how much street trees actually cool a neighbourhood. Advocacy materials throw around figures like ten degrees cooler, which I suspect is surface temperature under a single tree. What does the literature say about air temperature cooling at the block or neighbourhood scale as canopy cover increases?

**Assistant:** You are right to be suspicious of the headline numbers. Shaded surfaces can be 10 to 20 degrees cooler than sunlit pavement, but air temperature effects are much smaller. Studies using dense air temperature measurements typically find daytime cooling on the order of 0.5 to 2 degrees between low-canopy and high-canopy blocks, with the effect often nonlinear: cooling accelerates once canopy cover passes roughly 40 percent, because shading becomes continuous and evapotranspiration adds up. Effects also depend on tree species, irrigation, street orientation, and wind, and night-time effects can be weak or even slightly reversed under dense canopy that traps longwave radiation.

**User:** The nonlinearity is interesting. In our city most of the lower-income blocks are around ten or fifteen percent canopy, so if the real benefits only kick in near forty percent, planting a few trees here and there might do very little for heat. I'd like to estimate what it would actually take to move those blocks into the range where cooling becomes meaningful.

**Assistant:** That is a useful framing for planners. You could estimate the number of additional trees needed by working from crown area: divide the target canopy area increase by the expected mature crown area of suitable species, then adjust for mortality, since street trees in harsh sites often have high early loss. Be careful to note that mature canopy takes 15 to 25 years, so short-term heat relief may need complementary measures like cool pavements or shade structures. A scenario table with canopy targets, tree counts, and expected cooling ranges would make the trade-offs transparent.
