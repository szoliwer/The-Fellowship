**User:** We had a major bleaching event at four of our sites two years ago. Visually some reefs are starting to recover, coral cover is creeping back up. But the acoustic indices are kind of all over the place, and one recovering site actually has lower index values than before bleaching. I'm not sure if the soundscape is lagging the reef, or if the indices are just not capturing recovery at all. How do people interpret this?

**Assistant:** Both explanations are plausible. Fish communities can lag coral recovery because structure and recruitment take time, so the soundscape may genuinely be behind. But indices can also shift for unrelated reasons, such as changes in shrimp populations, boat traffic, or hydrophone sensitivity. It helps to break the soundscape into components: low-frequency fish bands, shrimp band, and anthropogenic noise, and look at each over time instead of a single index. Comparing with fish surveys at the same sites over the same period would show which explanation fits.

**User:** Breaking it into bands makes sense. Honestly, I'm becoming more sceptical of the whole idea that a single acoustic index can stand in for recovery. Everyone wants a simple reef health number from sound, and I'm not convinced it exists. I would rather measure something specific, like how often particular fish choruses occur, even if that's harder.

**Assistant:** That's a defensible position, and it's increasingly common among acoustic ecologists. Event-based measures, such as chorus occurrence, call rates of specific taxa, or the timing of dusk chorus onset, are more interpretable than broadband indices. They are harder to compute because they need detectors, but they connect directly to biological processes. You could present indices alongside these measures to show where they agree and where they diverge, which is informative in itself.
