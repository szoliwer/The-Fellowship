**User:** Following up on the fish call classification problem. I read a bit about self-supervised audio, things like contrastive learning and masked spectrogram modelling. It sounds like it could use all our unlabelled reef audio, which is the thing we have tons of. But I have no idea how much compute this needs or how to set it up. Would it be realistic for a small ecology lab with one shared GPU?

**Assistant:** It depends on scale. Training a large self-supervised audio model from scratch on tens of thousands of hours is a substantial compute job, typically many GPU-days. A more realistic path for a small lab is to start from an existing pretrained audio model and continue pretraining it on a subset of your reef audio, maybe a few hundred to a few thousand hours sampled to cover sites, seasons and times of day. Then you fine-tune on your labelled clips. That can fit on one good GPU over several days, but it does require someone comfortable with the training code and evaluation.

**User:** That's the bit I'm missing, the someone. My students are all field people too. I would honestly love to find an ML researcher who wants a real-world dataset with messy labels. We'd get a working call detector, they'd get a hard benchmark. I could even organise a few more expert labelling sessions to create a clean test set. Do you think that's an appealing pitch?

**Assistant:** It is a pretty appealing pitch. ML researchers often struggle to find realistic, large, domain-specific datasets with expert labels, and underwater bioacoustics is underexplored compared to birds. Emphasise the size and diversity of the archive, the existence of an expert-labelled test set, and clear tasks such as call detection and call-type classification. Offering co-authorship on a benchmark paper and a defined data-sharing agreement makes it easier for someone to commit time.
