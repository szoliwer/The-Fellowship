**User:** I have something like 30,000 hours of reef recordings and maybe 400 clips that someone has actually labelled to species or call type, mostly grunts and pops from damselfish and groupers. Manual annotation is never going to scale. I've tried a basic CNN on spectrograms and it overfits terribly. Honestly machine learning isn't my background, I'm a field ecologist. What approaches exist when you have this much unlabelled audio and so few labels?

**Assistant:** This is a classic low-label, high-volume setting, and there are a few families of approaches. Transfer learning from large pretrained audio models (trained on general sound or bird audio) often works well; you extract embeddings and train a small classifier on your 400 clips. Self-supervised learning lets a model learn representations from your 30,000 unlabelled hours first, then fine-tune on the labels. Few-shot methods like prototypical networks are designed for exactly a handful of examples per class. Active learning can also help: the model flags the most uncertain clips for you to label next, so each hour of annotation counts more.

**User:** The pretrained embedding route sounds the most doable for me, but I'm not sure models trained on birds or city sounds will transfer to underwater audio, which is so different, with the shrimp noise floor and all. I suspect I really need someone who actually works on audio ML to do this properly. I can provide the data and the ecological expertise about which calls matter, but I can't build the pipeline myself.

**Assistant:** Your concern is reasonable; underwater soundscapes have different noise statistics, and transfer is not guaranteed. A quick test is to compute embeddings from one or two pretrained models and check whether your labelled call types cluster at all. If they do, a light classifier may be enough. If not, self-supervised pretraining on your own archive is the stronger option, but it does need ML engineering time and GPUs. A collaborator from audio or bioacoustics ML would probably find your dataset very attractive, since large labelled-poor marine archives are rare and make good benchmarks.
