**User:** There's a lot of talk about applying high stack pressure to solid-state cells to keep the lithium in contact with the electrolyte. We see better cycling at higher pressure too. But I wonder whether pressure fixes the interface or just pushes lithium creep into voids and masks the problem, which would come back in a real pack where you can't apply that much pressure. Is there evidence either way?

**Assistant:** Both interpretations have support. Pressure promotes lithium creep, which fills voids formed during stripping and keeps the interface contact area high, lowering local current density and delaying dendrite initiation. But very high pressure can also drive lithium into cracks or pores in the electrolyte, which can actually accelerate shorting. Many lab results use pressures far above what's practical in commercial packs, so the improvements may not translate.

**User:** So it's double-edged. I'm curious whether there's a pressure window where contact is maintained without forcing lithium into grain boundaries. We have a small press rig and could run cells at several pressures. I'm not sure what range is meaningful though.

**Assistant:** A scan from low single-digit MPa up to around 10 to 20 MPa would cover the practically relevant and typical lab regimes. Track interfacial resistance, void formation after stripping, and time to short for each. If you see an optimum, it would be worth checking post-mortem whether higher pressures show more lithium intrusion at grain boundaries. Use at least three cells per pressure so you can tell a real trend from cell-to-cell scatter, and keep temperature constant, since lithium creep is strongly temperature dependent.
