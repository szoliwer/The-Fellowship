**User:** I'm working on garnet-type LLZO solid electrolytes with lithium metal anodes. We keep seeing short circuits at current densities well below what the bulk properties predict. Post-mortem SEM suggests lithium filaments running along grain boundaries rather than through grains. My working hypothesis is that grain boundaries have lower ionic conductivity and higher electronic conductivity, so lithium gets reduced inside them. Does that match what's known?

**Assistant:** Your hypothesis is consistent with a substantial line of thinking in the field. Grain boundaries in garnet electrolytes can have different chemistry, including lithium deficiency, segregated dopants or secondary phases, which may increase local electronic conductivity. That would allow lithium to nucleate inside the electrolyte rather than only at the interface. Others emphasise mechanical factors, such as stress concentration at boundaries, voids and cracks that lithium can wedge open. It's likely a combination, and the dominant mechanism may depend on processing and microstructure.

**User:** That fits with what we see. Our samples sintered at higher temperature have bigger grains and seem to last longer, but they also have more lithium loss, so it's confounded. I want to separate electronic leakage from mechanical cracking. I was thinking of measuring electronic conductivity with blocking electrodes on samples with different grain sizes. Is that a reasonable first step?

**Assistant:** Yes, DC polarisation with lithium-blocking electrodes is a sensible way to quantify electronic conductivity, and comparing grain sizes will help. To decouple grain boundary effects, you could also do impedance spectroscopy to separate bulk and grain boundary contributions, and control lithium loss by using mother powder during sintering. Correlating electronic conductivity and grain boundary density with critical current density would test your hypothesis directly.
