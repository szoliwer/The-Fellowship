**User:** I keep seeing headlines about the carbon footprint of training large language models, with numbers that vary by orders of magnitude. As someone who does applied empirical work, I'm skeptical whether these figures are even comparable. Different papers seem to use different assumptions about hardware, data centre efficiency, and the electricity grid. Is there a standard methodology, or is everyone kind of making up their own accounting?

**Assistant:** Your skepticism is justified. Most estimates multiply energy use by a carbon intensity factor, but choices vary at each step. Energy can come from measured power draw or be estimated from hardware specs and runtime. Data centre overhead, usually captured by power usage effectiveness, ranges from about 1.1 to 1.6. Carbon intensity depends on location and whether you use annual grid averages, hourly marginal emissions, or claimed renewable purchases. Some studies include hyperparameter tuning and failed runs, others count only the final run. Embodied emissions from manufacturing hardware are often excluded entirely.

**User:** So it's like comparing treatment effects across studies with different outcome definitions. A meta-analysis would need to harmonise everything first. I'd be curious whether someone could reconstruct a comparable estimate from published details, at least for the bigger models. Is the reporting detailed enough for that? And do inference costs end up mattering more than training once a model is deployed widely?

**Assistant:** Reconstruction is possible for some models, since papers often report GPU type, count, and training hours, but location and grid mix are frequently missing, and those can change results by a factor of five or more. Researchers have proposed reporting checklists to make this standard, but adoption is uneven. On inference, for widely used models, cumulative inference energy can exceed training energy over the deployment lifetime, though this depends heavily on usage volume and efficiency. That makes training-only comparisons a partial picture, which is another reason headline figures can mislead.
