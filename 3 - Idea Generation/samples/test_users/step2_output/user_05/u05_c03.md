**User:** Big worry in my class size project. If principals know the cap is 30, they might push enrolment around near the threshold. For example, a school with 31 kids might discourage one family or delay a transfer so they don't have to hire another teacher. Or some parents might deliberately pick schools just above the cutoff to get smaller classes. Either way the running variable is manipulated. How do I test for this convincingly?

**Assistant:** Start with a density test of the running variable at each cutoff. A classic density discontinuity test or the newer local polynomial density estimators will tell you whether there is bunching just below 30 or a gap just above it. Second, check covariate balance: prior test scores, share of low-income students, and parental education should be smooth through the cutoff. Third, think about timing. If enrolment is measured at a fixed date before schools can respond, manipulation is harder. Using an earlier enrolment measure, such as spring registrations, as the running variable can help.

**User:** I ran a quick density check and there's a visible pile-up at 29 and 30 and a dip at 31 and 32. Not huge but definitely there, and it's worse in schools with tight budgets. I'm thinking of using a donut RD that drops observations right at the threshold, and also using the previous year's projected enrolment as an instrument. I'm unsure whether the donut is enough or whether it just throws away the most informative observations.

**Assistant:** Your concern is valid. A donut RD only helps if manipulation is limited to observations right next to the cutoff. If schools near 33 also move students, the donut does not fix the problem, and you pay in precision and more extrapolation. Projected enrolment is a stronger idea if projections are made before schools can act strategically. You could also bound the effect, for example by trimming the excess mass below the cutoff in the most and least favourable ways. The heterogeneity by budget pressure is interesting in itself and may be worth reporting as evidence about the mechanism.
