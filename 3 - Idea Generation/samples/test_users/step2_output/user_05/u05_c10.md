**User:** Non-work question. I've kept a sourdough starter for about a year and I'm fascinated that after the first couple of chaotic weeks, it settles into a stable community. Early on it smelled weird and rose unpredictably, then it became consistent. What's actually happening? Why do the yeast and bacteria reach a stable mix instead of one taking over or the whole thing drifting randomly forever?

**Assistant:** Early on, flour and the environment bring in many microbes, including bacteria that do well at neutral pH. As lactic acid bacteria grow, they acidify the starter, which kills off many of those early colonisers. That is the strange smell phase. Once the pH is low, only acid-tolerant species thrive, typically a few lactic acid bacteria and one or two yeasts. Regular feeding resets resources but keeps the same selective conditions, so the community repeatedly converges on the same mix. The yeasts and bacteria also partly use different sugars, which reduces direct competition.

**User:** So it's a kind of environmental filtering with periodic disturbance. That makes me wonder how much of the final community depends on the flour versus the baker's hands or kitchen. If two people started starters from the same flour in different kitchens, would they converge to the same thing? It seems like a cute natural experiment, though I suspect people have looked at it.

**Assistant:** People have studied this. Comparisons of starters made from the same flour by different bakers suggest the flour contributes many of the microbes, but the baker's hands and environment also leave detectable signatures, and the feeding schedule and temperature strongly shape the final balance. So starters from the same flour often converge to similar but not identical communities. Temperature in particular shifts the ratio of yeast to bacteria and the balance of lactic and acetic acid, which is why the same starter can taste different in summer and winter.
