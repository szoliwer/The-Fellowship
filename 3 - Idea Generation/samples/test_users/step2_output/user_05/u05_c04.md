**User:** I've been thinking about maps lately. Everybody knows Mercator makes Greenland look as large as Africa, yet it's still the default in classrooms and on web maps. Why does it persist? And I'm curious whether growing up with Mercator actually distorts people's mental maps of the world, like do people really think northern countries are bigger? It seems like something you could measure, but I don't know if anyone has done it carefully.

**Assistant:** Mercator persists for a few reasons. It is conformal, so it preserves local angles and shapes, which made it ideal for navigation and now for web maps, where zooming into a street should not distort its shape. Web mapping tools adopted a variant of it because square tiles at every zoom level are easy to compute. Inertia matters too: textbooks and wall maps reuse familiar images. On mental maps, there is research where people draw or estimate country sizes, and many overestimate high-latitude regions and underestimate Africa, though disentangling Mercator exposure from other factors is tricky.

**User:** That's the part that interests me as an empirical person. You'd need some variation in exposure to Mercator that isn't correlated with everything else. Maybe classrooms that switched wall maps to an equal-area projection at some point? I'm not going to pursue it, but I'm curious how big the distortion is. Do people also misjudge distances, or is it mostly area?

**Assistant:** A curriculum change like that would be a natural experiment, though you would need to measure perceptions before and after. Studies suggest the area errors are the most consistent, especially overestimating Europe and North America relative to Africa and South America. Distance estimates are distorted too, but people tend to rely on travel time and other cues as well, so the projection effect is weaker. Interestingly, some research finds people's mental maps are not consistent with any single projection; they are a patchwork that blends map exposure, familiarity, and how culturally central a region feels.
