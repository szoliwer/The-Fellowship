**User:** I'm drafting a short opinion piece arguing that review should include reproducibility checks. My view is that someone on the review panel should actually run the code and confirm the data match what's reported. A colleague pushed back that reviewers are already overloaded. Can you help me think through who should carry that burden?

**Assistant:** A few options are worth laying out. Journals can hire data editors, which is reliable but costly. They can add a specialist reproducibility reviewer, often an early-career researcher who is paid or credited. They can require authors to supply containerized environments so checking is quick. Or they can use automated checks that confirm the code runs end to end and flag missing files. Many proposals combine author-side packaging with a dedicated checker, so the regular scientific reviewers aren't asked to do it on top of everything else.

**User:** A dedicated checker role feels right to me. The other angle I want in the piece is open review. If preprints are posted with code, then public commenters could also attempt reproduction, and that might spread the work around. I'm not sure whether open post-publication commenting on preprints actually leads to better papers though, or just more noise.

**Assistant:** That uncertainty is fair. Open commenting has clear benefits when commenters have relevant expertise and when authors respond, but participation is low and uneven, and quality varies. Crowdsourced reproduction attempts have worked in a few high-profile cases, mostly where a result was surprising enough to attract attention. For an opinion piece, you could present open review as a complement: formal reproducibility checks guarantee a minimum standard, while public commenting provides extra scrutiny for papers that draw interest.

**User:** Good, that framing avoids overclaiming. I'll say formal reproducibility checks are the floor and open preprint review is a possible bonus whose value we still haven't measured well. Maybe I'll close by saying someone should actually study whether public comments change the final paper.

**Assistant:** That's a strong closing because it turns an open question into a call for evidence. You could briefly sketch what such a study would look like, comparing preprints with and without substantive comments and tracking changes to methods or conclusions in the published version. Keeping the piece concrete like that tends to make opinion articles more persuasive to editors and readers.
