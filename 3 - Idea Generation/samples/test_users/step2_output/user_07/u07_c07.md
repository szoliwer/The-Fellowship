**User:** I've been reading about creole languages and I'm amazed that a full language with consistent grammar can apparently appear within a generation or two. Is that really true, and how? My naive take is that kids are doing the heavy lifting, regularising a messy pidgin, but I've also seen people argue the parent languages matter more than that.

**Assistant:** It's broadly true that creoles can stabilise quickly, though 'one generation' is debated. There are three main camps. The language bioprogram view, closest to your take, holds that children acquiring a pidgin impose innate grammatical defaults, which explains similarities across unrelated creoles. Substrate theories emphasise grammatical patterns carried over from the speakers' native languages. Superstrate views stress the colonial lexifier language. Many linguists now favour a mixture, with the balance depending on demographics, such as how many children were born in the setting and how much contact they had with each language.

**User:** The demographics part makes sense. I'd guess if there are lots of kids and little access to the colonial language, you'd get more child-driven innovation. Has anyone seen this happen in real time? It seems like it would be the cleanest test of the kids-do-it idea.

**Assistant:** The best-known real-time case is an emerging sign language that developed when deaf children were brought together in schools for the first time. Researchers documented that successive cohorts of children made the language more systematic, for example developing consistent spatial grammar that older signers used less reliably. That's often cited as support for your view that children regularise input. Critics note sign languages differ in modality and the community had no single parent language, so generalising to spoken creoles still needs care.
