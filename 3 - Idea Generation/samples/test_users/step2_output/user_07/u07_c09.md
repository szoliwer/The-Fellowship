**User:** Supporters of ranked-choice voting talk about it like it fixes everything. But Arrow's impossibility theorem says no ranked system can satisfy all of a handful of fairness criteria at once. So instant runoff must violate something. I think it's independence of irrelevant alternatives, but I'm also wondering whether Arrow even matters in practice or if it's just a theoretical curiosity.

**Assistant:** You're right that instant runoff can't escape Arrow; no ranked method with three or more candidates can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship together. IRV violates IIA, and it also fails monotonicity, meaning ranking a candidate higher can occasionally cause them to lose. It can also fail to elect a Condorcet winner, the candidate who beats every other head to head. Whether this matters in practice depends on how often such pathologies occur in real elections, which empirical studies suggest is uncommon but not negligible in close three-way races.

**User:** The monotonicity failure is the one that bugs me most, because it's so counterintuitive for voters. I still think IRV is better than plurality, but if we're redesigning anyway, why not use a Condorcet method? Or does score or approval voting dodge Arrow entirely since they aren't ranked?

**Assistant:** Cardinal methods like score and approval voting technically sit outside Arrow's framework because they use ratings rather than rankings, so they can satisfy IIA in a sense. They face other issues, such as strategic incentives about how to set approval thresholds, as described by the Gibbard–Satterthwaite line of results. Condorcet methods avoid IRV's centre-squeeze problem but can produce cycles needing a tiebreak rule, and they're harder to explain. Practical adoption often comes down to transparency and ease of counting as much as to theory.
