**User:** I've been curious about tidal-stream turbines. The resource is predictable, which seems great compared to wind, but I'm guessing maintenance underwater is brutal. In particular I wonder how much biofouling, like barnacles and algae on the blades, actually hurts performance. Is it a major cost driver or just a nuisance? My guess is it matters more than people admit.

**Assistant:** Your guess has support. Biofouling increases blade surface roughness, which can trigger early boundary-layer transition and flow separation, reducing lift and raising drag. Studies with artificial roughness on scaled blades have reported power coefficient losses ranging from a few percent to well over ten percent depending on severity. Fouling also adds mass, can affect seals and sensors, and complicates inspection. Combined with the cost of offshore vessel time for maintenance, it's a recognised operational cost driver rather than a minor nuisance.

**User:** Interesting. So what do people actually do about it? Antifouling paints with biocides seem like a bad idea in a sensitive marine environment, and I'd expect the high flow speeds to strip coatings anyway. Are there blade designs that are just more tolerant of roughness? That seems like the smarter route to me.

**Assistant:** Both routes are explored. Coatings have moved toward foul-release silicone and fluoropolymer surfaces that don't kill organisms but make attachment weak, so high flow speeds actually help shed growth. Durability under sediment erosion remains a concern, as you suspected. On design, engineers can choose airfoil sections with less sensitivity to leading-edge roughness, accepting slightly lower clean performance for better fouled performance. Retrievable nacelles that can be lifted for cleaning are another practical approach that reduces diving costs.
