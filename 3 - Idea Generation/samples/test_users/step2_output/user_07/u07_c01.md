**User:** Random question that's been bugging me. The big xenon direct-detection experiments keep getting bigger and keep reporting null results. At what point do we say WIMPs are dead? My gut says people keep moving the goalposts by just pushing the cross-section lower, but I honestly don't know if that's fair or if the parameter space really is that huge.

**Assistant:** It's a fair worry, but 'dead' is too strong. Null results exclude regions of the mass versus cross-section plane, and the classic electroweak-scale WIMP with a Z-boson-mediated coupling is indeed largely ruled out. However, many WIMP models predict much weaker spin-independent couplings, for example Higgs-mediated or loop-suppressed interactions, which sit below current limits. The real wall is the neutrino fog: below a certain cross-section, solar and atmospheric neutrinos produce nuclear recoils indistinguishable from dark matter. Experiments are approaching that floor now, so the next generation is somewhat of a last clean shot for heavy WIMPs using this technique.

**User:** Okay, the neutrino fog thing actually makes me feel better about the goalpost complaint, because at least there's a natural stopping point. I still think the field oversold WIMPs as the obvious answer for decades though. Is there anything besides directional detectors that could see past the fog, or is that basically where direct detection ends?

**Assistant:** Directional detection is the most cited route, since neutrinos from the Sun come from a known direction while a dark matter wind would come from the direction of Earth's motion through the halo. Other options include using different target nuclei, because neutrino and dark matter recoil spectra scale differently with nucleus type, and long-exposure annual modulation studies. Your point about overselling is shared by many physicists; the 'WIMP miracle' was elegant, and it arguably crowded out attention to axions and lighter candidates, which are now getting much more experimental effort.
