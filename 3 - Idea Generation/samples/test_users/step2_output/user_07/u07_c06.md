**User:** Every popular science thing I read says trees share sugar with their kids through fungal networks and warn each other about pests. It sounds lovely but I'm getting suspicious it's overstated. I read somewhere that a lot of the field studies have small sample sizes. How solid is the evidence that mature trees actually transfer meaningful resources to seedlings through mycorrhizal networks?

**Assistant:** Your suspicion is shared by a number of ecologists. Recent reviews have argued that three popular claims are weakly supported: that common mycorrhizal networks are widespread in forests, that resources move through them in ecologically meaningful amounts, and that mature trees preferentially help offspring. Many isotope-labelling studies show some carbon moves between trees, but often can't rule out alternative pathways through soil, and the amounts are usually small. Fungi themselves are not altruistic intermediaries; they likely move carbon according to their own needs.

**User:** That's sort of what I expected. I think the 'mother tree' framing really anthropomorphises a fungus doing whatever is good for the fungus. Still, I don't want to throw it all out. What would a convincing experiment even look like? I imagine you'd need to physically confirm a single fungal network connects two trees and then trace the label through only that link.

**Assistant:** That's essentially the design critics have called for. A convincing study would genetically confirm the same fungal individual colonises both trees, use mesh barriers with pore sizes that block hyphae versus allow them to separate network transfer from soil diffusion, and label one tree with isotopes while measuring uptake in the receiver's own tissues, not just its roots. Replication across many tree pairs and forest types matters too. Few studies meet all of these conditions, which is why the debate remains open.
