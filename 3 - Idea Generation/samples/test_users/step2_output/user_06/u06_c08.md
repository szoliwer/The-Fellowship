**User:** Coffee question. I've been trying to understand why grind size matters so much for espresso. Finer grind obviously means more surface area, but I've read that grinding too fine actually reduces extraction because of channeling. That seems counterintuitive. What's the physics of water flowing through a coffee puck, and why would finer particles cause uneven flow?

**Assistant:** The puck acts like a porous medium, and flow follows the standard linear law for porous media: flow rate depends on pressure and permeability. Finer grinds lower permeability, so water moves slower and contacts more surface area, increasing extraction up to a point. Channeling happens because a puck is never perfectly uniform. If one region is slightly more permeable, more water flows through it, which extracts and erodes it faster, making it even more permeable. This positive feedback creates channels, while other regions are bypassed. Very fine grinds make the puck more sensitive to these instabilities, so overall extraction can fall.

**User:** That positive feedback loop makes sense, it's like current finding the path of least resistance and widening it. So distribution techniques and tamping are basically trying to make the initial permeability as uniform as possible. Does the size distribution of particles matter as much as the average size? I've read about fines migrating and clogging the bottom of the puck.

**Assistant:** That analogy fits well. The distribution matters a lot. Grinders produce a range of particle sizes, and fines can migrate downward during brewing, clogging the bottom of the puck or the filter and creating uneven resistance. A wide distribution also means fines over-extract while larger particles under-extract, which affects taste. Grinders that produce narrower distributions tend to allow finer grind settings before channeling sets in. Practices like pre-infusion help too, because wetting the puck at low pressure lets it swell evenly before full pressure is applied.
