**User:** I've been trying to simulate our pneumatic fingers in FEA before printing molds, but the simulations don't match reality. We use a two-term polynomial hyperelastic model with parameters from the datasheet for our silicone, and the simulated bending angle at a given pressure is off by 20 to 30 percent. I suspect the material parameters, but there's also the cast quality, air bubbles, and wall thickness variation. Where would you start debugging the sim-to-real gap?

**Assistant:** I would start by characterising your own silicone rather than relying on datasheet values. Cure conditions, mixing ratio, and degassing all change stiffness. Run uniaxial tensile tests on samples cast in the same batch as the fingers, and ideally also a biaxial or planar test, since a model fit only to uniaxial data can mispredict other deformation modes. Next, measure the actual wall thicknesses of a sectioned finger, because small variations strongly affect bending. Finally, check boundary conditions and whether your model includes contact between chambers, which matters at higher pressures.

**User:** We did a quick tensile test and the real silicone was about 15 percent stiffer than the datasheet, which explains part of it. Wall thickness also varies by up to half a millimetre because the mold pieces shift. I'm wondering whether it's better to calibrate the model per batch or just accept the sim as rough and do more physical testing. Also, does viscoelasticity matter for slow gripping?

**Assistant:** Calibrating against a few physical tests per batch is a common compromise: fit a correction or update the material parameters so the simulation matches measured bending, then use the model to explore design changes. It saves printing many molds. For wall thickness, improving mold alignment with registration pins may help more than better modelling. Viscoelasticity matters somewhat even for slow gripping, since silicone relaxes under sustained load, so contact forces drift during holding. If you hold fruit for several seconds, a simple viscoelastic model or a hold test could explain discrepancies in grip force over time.
