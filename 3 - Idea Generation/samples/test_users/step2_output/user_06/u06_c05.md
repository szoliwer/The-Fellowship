**User:** We're now also prototyping a pneumatic soft gripper alongside the fin-ray one, three silicone fingers with PneuNet chambers, for raspberries specifically. The pneumatic version seems gentler because it conforms more, but it's slower and it needs a compressor and valves on the robot, which our harvesting platform doesn't have. I'm trying to figure out what metrics to compare them on fairly before we commit to one.

**Assistant:** A fair comparison could include: bruising rate measured at 24 and 48 hours after picking; picking success rate, including drops and missed grasps; cycle time per fruit; damage to neighbouring fruit or plants; robustness to variation in fruit size and ripeness; and system cost and complexity, including power and compressed air. It also helps to measure peak contact pressure for both designs on standardised dummy fruit, so you can relate design choices to bruising. Since ripeness matters a lot, try to stratify tests by firmness rather than mixing all fruit together.

**User:** That list is helpful. Cycle time is the one where I suspect the pneumatic gripper loses badly, about 1.8 seconds per grasp versus 0.7 for fin-ray. But honestly these numbers come from our lab and I don't know what growers would consider acceptable. A gentle gripper that's too slow is useless commercially. I'd love to run both on a real picking line for a season and get feedback from the people who actually handle the fruit.

**Assistant:** That is a key point: acceptable performance depends on the economics of the operation, which lab tests cannot capture. Growers will care about throughput, the value of the fruit, labour availability, and how much bruising downgrades a punnet. A season-long trial would also show durability issues, such as silicone tearing or TPU fatigue, that short tests miss. When you approach a grower, offering a side-by-side comparison with human pickers on bruising and speed could make the trial more attractive, since it gives them useful data too.
