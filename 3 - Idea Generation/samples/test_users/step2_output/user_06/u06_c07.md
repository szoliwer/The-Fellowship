**User:** Came across videos of acoustic levitation, where tiny beads float in the nodes of standing ultrasound waves. A colleague said it could be used for contactless manipulation of small parts in assembly. I'm curious how the physics works and what the practical limits are. How big and heavy an object can you levitate, and can you actually move objects precisely, not just hold them in place?

**Assistant:** Acoustic levitation uses the acoustic radiation force: in a standing wave, small objects experience a net force pushing them toward pressure nodes. Typical setups use ultrasonic transducers around 40 kHz, which gives a wavelength of about 8.5 mm, so objects smaller than about half the wavelength are easiest to trap. Dense materials like small metal parts are harder but possible with stronger fields. To move objects, phased arrays of many transducers can shift the position of the nodes by changing relative phases, allowing objects to be translated, rotated, and even combined in mid-air.

**User:** So the wavelength basically sets the size limit, and you'd need higher frequency for smaller objects and lower frequency for larger ones. I'm guessing there's a trade-off with force and stability. Could this ever handle something soft or fragile, like a cell cluster or a droplet, without damaging it? That seems like the real advantage of not touching things at all.

**Assistant:** Yes, frequency sets the scale, and higher frequencies attenuate more in air, which limits trapping strength at a distance. Fragile objects are a real strength of the method. Droplets, small biological samples, and delicate particles have been levitated, and it avoids contamination from contact with surfaces. There are caveats: strong acoustic fields can heat or deform droplets, cause streaming flows, or make them oscillate. For biological material, researchers typically work in liquid using acoustofluidics, where similar principles move cells gently in microchannels.
