**User:** I watched a documentary on migratory birds and they mentioned that birds might see magnetic fields through some quantum effect in their eyes. That sounds wild. I'm an engineer, so my instinct is to ask what the actual sensor is. What is the radical-pair hypothesis, and how would a chemical reaction in the retina give a bird directional information? It seems way too noisy to work.

**Assistant:** The radical-pair hypothesis proposes that light hitting a protein called cryptochrome in the retina creates a pair of molecules with unpaired electrons. Their spins are correlated and oscillate between two states, singlet and triplet, and the Earth's magnetic field slightly changes the rate of that interconversion depending on the molecule's orientation relative to the field. Since the two states lead to different chemical products, the reaction yield varies with direction. With many cryptochromes aligned in the retina, the bird might perceive a pattern overlaid on its vision that shifts as it turns its head.

**User:** OK so it's essentially an array of orientation-dependent sensors and the brain does the signal processing. That's actually a neat design. What still bugs me is how the spin coherence survives long enough in a warm, wet cell. In engineering we'd call that a terrible operating environment. Is there experimental evidence, or is it mostly theoretical plausibility?

**Assistant:** That is exactly the main open question. For the effect to work, the spin states need to stay coherent for roughly a microsecond, which is long for a biological system. Evidence is mixed but suggestive: birds lose their magnetic orientation under weak radiofrequency fields at frequencies that would disrupt radical pairs, while ordinary magnets don't explain it. Laboratory studies show cryptochrome from migratory birds is magnetically sensitive in vitro. Still, nobody has directly measured the effect in a living bird's retina, so the full chain from molecule to behaviour remains partly inferred.
