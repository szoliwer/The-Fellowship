**User:** I've been playing with origami patterns as inspiration for deployable structures, and I keep seeing the term rigid foldability. As far as I understand, it means a crease pattern can fold with the faces staying flat and only bending at the creases. Why do some patterns like Miura-ori fold rigidly and others don't? Is there a simple condition I can check, or is it a hard computational problem?

**Assistant:** Rigid foldability means the pattern can move continuously from flat to folded with each face staying rigid, like metal plates connected by hinges. For a single vertex, the conditions are fairly well understood: angles around the vertex and the assignment of mountain and valley folds must be compatible. For a whole crease pattern, every vertex has to fold consistently with its neighbours, which creates a system of constraints around each loop of faces. Miura-ori works because its vertices are all degree four with a repeating geometry, giving a single degree of freedom. Deciding rigid foldability in general is computationally hard.

**User:** Interesting, so degree-four vertices are kind of the sweet spot because they give one degree of freedom. For a deployable structure that's ideal, since one actuator can drive the whole thing. Practically, though, real sheets have thickness. Does thickness break rigid foldability, and how do people deal with it in real panels?

**Assistant:** Exactly, a single degree of freedom is very attractive for actuation. Thickness is a real problem: with thick panels the hinges cannot all lie in the same plane, so the ideal kinematics fail and panels collide. Common solutions include offsetting hinges to the panel edges, tapering panels so they nest when folded, and the membrane approach where thin hinges connect thick panels with gaps. There are design methods that preserve the kinematics of the original pattern while accommodating thickness. Each comes with trade-offs in folded volume, weight, and manufacturing complexity.
